I teach at the intersection of statistical theory and real-world application, with an emphasis on social science and public health contexts. I have taught at all levels, from introductory statistics to advanced data science, across four institutions in three countries.

Curriculum design is a large part of why I do this work. After my postdoc I chose a position at Reed College, a liberal arts college whose reputation rests on undergraduate teaching, and took the opportunity there to work on my own practice with a focus on inclusive pedagogy. Since then I have rebuilt all of my modules from scratch. I am a Fellow of Advance HE (FHEA).

## Current courses, University of Southampton

### RESM3001: Applied Social Quantitative Analyses

Module lead · autumn 2024, 2025 & 2026 · final-year undergraduate, semester 1

In 2026/27 I teach the whole module myself. The lecture is on Monday, the computer workshop on Thursday, and a short quiz on Moodle closes each week. Assessment combines seven quizzes (35%), a group project on a UK dataset (30%) and a closed-book exam (35%).

**Topics covered**

- Statistics, society and measurement: the history of quantification and the concepts of validity and reliability
- Measurement in practice, what and who: gross domestic product, mortality and conflict counts, and who is counted
- Measurement in practice, how: surveys, fieldwork, and simple random, stratified and cluster sampling; design effects and survey weights in R
- Linear regression: simple and multiple regression, inference, and interpretation through predicted values
- Causal reasoning: directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), confounding, mediation and colliders, with quasi-experiments at a conceptual level
- Logistic regression for binary outcomes: log-odds, odds ratios, predicted probabilities and effect plots
- Multinomial logistic regression for nominal outcomes, and ordinal logistic regression for ordered outcomes
- Text as data: how a language model or text classifier turns words into categories, and how to evaluate machine-coded labels
- Group project presentations and exam review

**How I teach it**

I teach this module as a course in how numbers about society are made and then modelled. The first weeks read official statistics and survey fieldwork critically, using work such as Desrosières, Jerven and Biruk, and the same questions about who is counted and how return in every later model. All analysis runs in R and Quarto from the first workshop, so students leave with a reproducible workflow they can reuse in a dissertation or a job.

Each model family is introduced through the question it answers. I choose the model from the type of the outcome, and I ask students to read results as predicted probabilities and effect plots in plain words. DAGs give them a shared way to say what a model adjusts for and why.

Students write down what they expect a result to be before they estimate it, and the quizzes and the project reward explaining the gap between the expectation and the estimate. Students may use generative AI tools to write R code, and they declare that use. They must be able to explain every block of code they run. Marks go to interpretation and none go to code, and the exam is closed book. I designed this for a cohort with ready access to language models, so that assessment measures what students understand about the data.

### RESM2001: Introduction to Social Data Analytics

Module lead · spring 2025 & 2026 · second-year undergraduate, semester 2, around 200 students

The module teaches the statistical foundations of quantitative social science through lectures, pre-recorded explanations, weekly computer workshops and tutorials. In 2025/26 I led its complete migration from SPSS to R, covering coursework, lectures, tutorials, assessment and solutions. Moving the module onto open-source tools removes a licence barrier and leaves students with software they can keep after graduation.

**Topics covered**

- Z-scores and the normal distribution
- From sample to population: sampling distributions, the Central Limit Theorem and standard errors
- Confidence intervals
- Hypothesis tests about means: one sample, two independent samples and paired samples
- Analysis of variance (ANOVA), post-hoc comparisons and assumptions
- Tables and chi-squared tests for categorical data, and tests for proportions
- Simple linear regression, with confidence intervals and hypothesis tests for the coefficients
- Multiple linear regression, checking assumptions, and categorical explanatory variables
- Non-parametric methods: sign, rank-sum and signed-rank tests, Kruskal-Wallis, and Spearman correlation
- Choosing the right test for a research question
- Revision sessions

**How I teach it**

I teach this module as a first experience of working with real data. Each weekly computer workshop analyses an existing UK survey dataset, so students meet missing values, labelled categories and skewed distributions from the start. The workshops use one consistent R workflow built on the tidyverse and tidymodels, which I chose so that students read the same code style in every week and can transfer it to later modules.

The early workshops are interactive tutorials built with learnr, which check each step as students type. Later workshops use scripts in R Markdown, which prepare students to write their own reproducible analyses for the assignment.

I place the emphasis on interpretation. Each test and model is taught through the research question it answers, the assumptions behind it, and the sentence a social scientist would write about the result. For that reason the module ends with a decision flowchart for choosing among the tests, and the exam asks students to interpret provided R output in words.
