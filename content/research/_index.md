My research lies at the intersection of Bayesian methods, spatial statistics, small-area estimation, survey methodology, and official statistics. One question runs through all of it: how do we measure health, poverty, and access to services where data are sparse, contested, or expensive to collect, and how do we build tools that ministries of health and statistical offices can own and use?

It falls into three main strands: measuring health where data are sparse; the production of official statistics under machine learning; and survey methodology and the measurement of inequality.

I collaborate with the World Health Organization, UNAIDS, Gavi, UNICEF, the World Bank, and national programmes, most often on HIV and immunisation coverage across African countries. I am a member of the UNAIDS Reference Group on Estimates, Modelling and Projections.

## Measuring health where data are sparse

### Calibrated estimation from routine data

For two decades, subnational estimates of health in many African countries have rested on large, donor-funded household surveys. As that funding shrinks, a pressing question is whether the routine data that countries already collect and own, through their health information systems, can carry more of the load. Routine facility data are abundant and timely but biased, because the people who appear in them are not a random sample; surveys are unbiased by design but sparse and infrequent. With Li-Chun Zhang, I develop a register-based framework that treats routine data as the primary signal and uses a sparse probability survey to calibrate its bias. The same structural idea covers two very different problems: selection bias in routine HIV testing, and denominator bias in administrative immunisation coverage. I presented the framework at the Conference on Small Area Estimation (SAE 2026) and at the S3RI Research Festival 2026, and I will present it at a workshop of the Italian National Institute of Statistics (ISTAT) in December 2026.

### HIV epidemiology and the care cascade

I build models that combine population-based surveys, programmatic data, and routine health information systems to describe the HIV epidemic and the care cascade. Recent work covers disparities in HIV prevalence, testing, and treatment coverage across 33 African countries, temporal trends in antenatal HIV testing, and the scale-up of HIV self-testing across eight national programmes.

### Immunisation coverage and geographic inequalities

Geographic inequalities in childhood vaccination remain large, and they are sensitive to how we measure them. I work on small-area methods for immunisation coverage that draw on Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS), and administrative data, with attention to the assumptions hidden inside common indicators, and in particular to the denominator: the target population that administrative coverage divides by is often wrong, sometimes badly enough to push reported coverage above 100%. A recent scoping review, with colleagues at WHO, Gavi, and UNICEF, maps how spatial methods are used across this literature and where they still depend on survey data.

## The production of official statistics under machine learning

I'm interested in what happens to official statistics when machine-learning methods enter their production: at coding and classification, at the integration of administrative and alternative data, and at estimation. A methodological strand, with Paul Smith, frames "algorithm-assisted inference" as a continuity with model-assisted estimation. A companion strand studies the same transition ethnographically, and I presented it at the Royal Statistical Society International Conference (RSS 2026) in the talk "Why Ethnography? Studying AI adoption in official statistics as a problem of scientific practice".

## Survey methodology and the measurement of inequality

A recurring question in my work is whether widely used survey instruments measure what we think they measure.

### The wealth index and socio-economic status

I have presented work on the DHS wealth index as a proxy for socio-economic status in low-resource settings at the Population Association of America (PAA) meeting 2025 and at the Italian Conference on Survey Methodology (ITACOSM) 2025, where I also presented a comparison of asset-based wealth and income or consumption poverty in Mozambique.

### The politics of quantification

I am increasingly drawn to questions about how numbers govern. From September 2026 I supervise a PhD project on the politics of indicators in global health, reviewing PEPFAR data and policies over the last decade.

## Current projects

### Counting the Uncounted: statistics, digital health and immunisation equity

A University of Southampton Global Partnership Award for Africa (£9,016, to July 2027), of which I am the principal investigator. The project develops a statistical framework for small-area estimates of immunisation coverage, with quantified uncertainty, in Mozambique and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. It works with the Mozambique Ministry of Health and the Kinshasa School of Public Health, and it funds a workshop in Maputo.

### Review of sampling and weighting for Scottish Government surveys

A £40,000 contract from the Scottish Government, from October 2026 to March 2027, for a methodological review of the sampling and weighting used in its flagship population surveys. Paul Smith leads the Southampton team and I am a co-investigator. The review diagnoses the current methods and sets out options for future practice.
