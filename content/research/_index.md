My research lies at the intersection of Bayesian methods, spatial statistics, small-area estimation, survey methodology, and official statistics. One question runs through all of it: how do we measure health, poverty, and access to services where data are sparse, contested, or expensive to collect, and how do we build tools that ministries of health and statistical offices can own and use?

Most of this work happens with partners: the World Health Organization, the Mozambique Ministry of Health, the Kinshasa School of Public Health, Bluesquare, and the UNAIDS Reference Group on Estimates, Modelling and Projections, of which I am a member. The [partners](#partners-heading) section at the foot of this page links to each of them.

{{< strand n="1" title="Measuring health where data are sparse" lead="Combining surveys, programme data and routine health information to estimate HIV, immunisation and quality of care below the national level." >}}

{{< project title="Calibrated estimation from routine data" mark="routine data" with="With Li-Chun Zhang (University of Southampton)" >}}
For two decades, subnational estimates of health in many African countries have rested on large, donor-funded household surveys. As that funding shrinks, the routine data that countries already collect and own, through their health information systems, need to provide more of the evidence. Routine facility data are abundant and timely but biased, because the people who appear in them are not a random sample; surveys are unbiased by design but sparse and infrequent. We develop a register-based framework that treats routine data as the primary signal and uses a small probability survey to calibrate its bias, for selection bias in routine HIV testing and denominator bias in administrative immunisation coverage. I presented the framework at the Conference on Small Area Estimation (SAE 2026) and the S3RI Research Festival 2026, and I will present it at a workshop of the Italian National Institute of Statistics (ISTAT) in December 2026.
{{< /project >}}

{{< project title="HIV epidemiology and the care cascade" img="/publications/socio-demographic-disparities-hiv-33-african-countries" with="With the WHO, the Mozambique Ministry of Health and national HIV programmes in eight countries" papers="/publications/socio-demographic-disparities-hiv-33-african-countries|/publications/scaling-up-hiv-self-testing-africa|/publications/temporal-trends-hiv-testing-antenatal-ssa" >}}
I build models that combine population-based surveys, programmatic data, and routine health information systems to describe the HIV epidemic and the care cascade: disparities in HIV prevalence, testing and treatment coverage across 33 African countries, trends in antenatal HIV testing, and the scale-up of HIV self-testing across eight national programmes.
{{< /project >}}

{{< project title="Immunisation coverage and geographic inequalities" img="/publications/geographic-inequalities-immunisation-scoping-review" with="With colleagues at the WHO, Gavi and UNICEF" papers="/publications/geographic-inequalities-immunisation-scoping-review|/publications/inequality-childhood-immunization-scoping-review|/publications/wealth-related-inequality-tetanus-vaccination" >}}
Geographic inequalities in childhood vaccination remain large, and they are sensitive to how we measure them. I work on small-area methods for immunisation coverage that draw on Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS), and administrative data, with attention to the denominator: the target population that administrative coverage divides by is often wrong, sometimes badly enough to push reported coverage above 100%.
{{< /project >}}

{{< project title="Quality of care in health facilities" img="/publications/small-area-model-healthcare-quality" papers="/publications/small-area-model-healthcare-quality" >}}
Health facility surveys measure whether services are ready and whether care follows guidelines, but they sample few facilities in each region. I developed a small-area model that pools these surveys over time to track readiness and process quality at subnational level, applied to Kenya, Senegal and Tanzania from 1999 to 2020.
{{< /project >}}

{{< strand n="2" title="Official statistics and machine learning" lead="What happens to official statistics when machine-learning methods enter their production." >}}

{{< project title="Algorithm-assisted inference" mark="official statistics" with="With Paul Smith (University of Southampton)" papers="/publications/algorithm-assisted-inference-official-statistics" >}}
Machine learning now enters official statistics at coding and classification, at the integration of administrative and alternative data, and at estimation. We frame "algorithm-assisted inference" as a continuity with model-assisted estimation. A companion strand studies the same transition ethnographically, and I presented it at the Royal Statistical Society International Conference (RSS 2026) in the talk "Why Ethnography? Studying AI adoption in official statistics as a problem of scientific practice".
{{< /project >}}

{{< project title="The wealth index and socio-economic status" mark="wealth index" >}}

{{< project title="The politics of quantification" mark="indicators" with="PhD project of [Clare Mitchell](/supervision/), from September 2026" >}}
I am increasingly drawn to questions about how numbers govern. From September 2026 I supervise a PhD project on the politics of indicators in global health, reviewing PEPFAR data and policies over the last decade.
